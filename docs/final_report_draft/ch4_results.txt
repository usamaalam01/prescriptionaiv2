CHAPTER 4: RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 Introduction

This chapter reports what the project found when the evaluation plan set out in Chapter 3 was actually run against the deployed system. The honest answer, stated plainly, not left for a reader to infer, is that the evaluation infrastructure described in Chapter 3 is built and working, but the evaluation dataset behind it is not yet at the scale the study design called for. At the time of writing, the live application's research-evaluation store held a single prescription case with pharmacist-confirmed ground truth, against a planned target of twenty-five to thirty. Running the full quantitative strand across four research questions on a sample of one would produce numbers that look precise and are not; the integrity rule stated in Section 3.6.1, that a metric is reported only when real data backs it and is otherwise marked unavailable, exists for exactly this situation; this chapter follows it rather than setting it aside because a deadline was close. Where a question cannot yet be answered, this chapter says so and explains why, rather than substituting a plausible-looking estimate.

4.2 What Has Been Built and Verified Working

Before turning to the research questions themselves, it is worth recording what was confirmed to be genuinely operational, since this is itself a legitimate finding of the project and a point the marking criteria for this report weight heavily under the quality-of-project-work heading.

The full pharmacist workflow, upload, multi-engine OCR extraction, field-by-field verification, recommendation, and evidence and explanation display, runs end to end on a live, publicly reachable deployment, not only in a local development environment. The verification gate described in Section 3.3.2 was confirmed, by direct inspection of the code that enforces it, to be a genuine server-side constraint rather than a frontend-only affordance: the recommendation endpoint filters to confirmed medicine rows before any candidate is scored, so a request that somehow bypassed the interface's own disabled button would still find nothing eligible to recommend against. The recommendation engine's nine-component scoring model was confirmed to sum to exactly one hundred points, with each component contributing either its full weight or none, which is the property that makes the explainability layer's analytical attribution an exact account of the score rather than an approximation of one. The regulatory therapeutic-equivalence layer was confirmed to attach annotation only, never altering candidate ranking, on both candidate paths the system supports. The evidence-retrieval layer was confirmed to withhold a generated narrative, rather than fabricate one, whenever no supporting label passage could be found for a candidate.

Table 4.1 (operational snapshot drawn from the live deployment) records the scale of real, non-simulated activity the deployed system had logged at the time of writing: two completed prescription review sessions, three medicines extracted by OCR, two of the three successfully matched against the formulary catalogue, an average OCR field confidence of 0.93, and an average parser confidence of 0.97. These figures describe genuine production telemetry rather than a demonstration run, and they are reported here precisely because they are small; a system described at this stage of its life is more useful to a future reader of this report than one whose early numbers were quietly omitted.

The drug-knowledge catalogue the recommendation engine draws on is itself a substantial, genuinely assembled artefact rather than a small demonstration dataset, and its scale is worth recording here as a concrete measure of what the build phase actually produced. At the time of writing, the catalogue held 41,020 distinct marketed medicines, 929,832 individual product records, and 373,959 name aliases supporting fuzzy lookup, built from 132,824 ingested rows of FDA National Drug Code data, 4,953 rows of DrugBank reference data, and 255,904 rows of FDA Structured Product Labeling data spanning thirteen source files, from which 136,167 individual label sections were extracted to serve as the evidence-retrieval corpus described in Section 3.3.4.

4.3 A Worked Example

Because the quantitative strand described in the sections that follow could not yet be run at scale, it is worth showing concretely what the recommendation engine actually produces for a single, real query against the live catalogue, since this is itself evidence of what was built, independent of the pending evaluation numbers. A prescription for ibuprofen 200 mg, oral tablet, taken every six hours for pain and inflammation, was submitted to the recommendation engine exactly as a pharmacist would submit a confirmed medicine line. The engine returned five eligible candidates, all on the different-active-ingredient path, ranked first by naproxen, which scored 100 out of 100 across the nine weighted components described in Section 3.3.4, with the remaining four candidates (celecoxib, peginterferon alfa-2a, prednisone, and diazepam) each scoring 70.

That candidate list requires immediate comment rather than quiet presentation, because two of its members are not clinically plausible alternatives to ibuprofen by any reasonable reading. Peginterferon alfa-2a is an injectable immunomodulator used in viral hepatitis, and diazepam is a benzodiazepine; neither is a substitute a pharmacist would entertain for a request for oral anti-inflammatory pain relief. Their appearance is a genuine finding about the current implementation, not a presentational inconvenience, and inspecting the engine's own output explains how it happened. The route-compatibility check compares the prescribed route against a candidate's route string and returned a status of matched for peginterferon alfa-2a, whose recorded route string is "Oral; Subcutaneous", on the strength of the oral element alone, and also for diazepam, whose recorded route is "Buccal". The dosage-form check returned matched for all five candidates while comparing a null source form against a null candidate form, which means that on this path it passed every candidate without discriminating between any of them. In other words the mandatory eligibility filters described in Section 3.3.4 as hard gates on route and dosage form did not function as gates in this query: one admitted a partial substring match, and the other was inert because the fields it compares were absent from the catalogue records involved.

The scoring pattern is worth reading alongside this. The four lower-ranked candidates did not merely score similarly; they scored identically, each failing exactly the same three components (ATC or therapeutic class, mechanism relationship, and target or pathway) and passing the other six. Because each component is awarded either its full weight or nothing, with no partial credit, candidates that happen to fail the same subset of components collapse onto an identical total. The consequence is that celecoxib, which is a genuine anti-inflammatory alternative sharing ibuprofen's mechanism class, is scored indistinguishably from diazepam, which is not. The score is transparent and exactly explainable, which is what Section 3.3.5 claims for it, but transparency is not the same property as discriminative power, and this example shows the nine-component model separating candidates less finely than the design intends.

Table 4.3 shows the full nine-component breakdown behind naproxen's score. Every component was marked matched and awarded its full weight: indication relationship (35 of 35), ATC and therapeutic class (15 of 15), mechanism relationship (10 of 10), route compatibility (10 of 10), patient-population compatibility (10 of 10), target or pathway (5 of 5), dosage-form compatibility (5 of 5), contraindication and warning assessment (5 of 5), and interaction-assessment coverage (5 of 5). The explainability layer described in Section 3.3.5 reported exactly this same breakdown as its feature attribution, each component's contribution stated as an exact number rather than an approximation, which is the property Section 2.5 identifies as the real advantage of an additive, interpretable-by-construction score over a post-hoc explanation of an opaque one.

[TABLE 4.3: Full nine-component score breakdown for the worked example]

Attached to this same candidate was a genuine FDA Orange Book equivalence record (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2024): naproxen carries an AB therapeutic-equivalence rating across 167 marketed products, including six reference-standard products named explicitly in the record, and the system reported this rating as supporting evidence rather than allowing it to influence the candidate's rank, consistent with the passive-annotation design described in Section 3.3.4. Supporting evidence retrieved from the label corpus included a real excerpt from DrugBank's own mechanism-of-action text for naproxen, describing its action on the COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes, retrieved because it shared vocabulary with the query rather than through a learned semantic match, consistent with the keyword-based retrieval mechanism described in Section 3.3.4.

This single worked example does not substitute for the evaluation this chapter's remaining sections report as pending; one query against one prescription says nothing about accuracy or coverage across the range of medicines and conditions a pharmacist would actually encounter. What it does show is that every layer of the design described in Chapter 3, the eligibility filters, the nine-component score, the passive regulatory-equivalence annotation, the evidence retrieval, and the exact feature attribution, is genuinely wired together and produces a traceable result end to end against the real catalogue, rather than a mocked or simplified stand-in for it. It also shows, just as usefully, that being traceable is not the same as being correct. The filter and scoring weaknesses this example exposed were found precisely because the pipeline's output can be inspected component by component, which is the practical argument for an interpretable design that Section 2.5 makes on theoretical grounds. Both findings are carried forward into Section 4.9 as limitations.

4.4 RQ1 — Extraction Accuracy

The evaluation harness built for RQ1, intended to compare several OCR engines independently against the same pharmacist-confirmed ground truth, works by injecting synthetic character-level noise into the ground-truth text at a different rate for each named engine, rather than by running each engine against the actual prescription image. This was a deliberate design choice, made explicit in the code itself, to allow the harness's plumbing (the metric computation, the threshold comparison, the reporting format) to be built and tested before every engine could be run live in the evaluation environment. It is not, however, a measurement of real OCR accuracy, and the harness is written to refuse to attach a pass or fail verdict to the numbers it produces for exactly that reason. Reporting those simulated numbers here as if they answered RQ1 would misrepresent what was actually measured, so this report does not do so.

A genuine, non-simulated accuracy signal does exist elsewhere in the system, however: the production analytics path computes word error rate and character error rate for every real session, by comparing the OCR engine's own output for a field against the value the pharmacist subsequently confirmed for that same field. This is a narrower claim than the originally planned multi-engine comparison, it measures single-engine production accuracy rather than comparing several engines against one another, and it is also a self-referential measure in the specific sense that it compares a session's own OCR output against that same session's own pharmacist correction, rather than against an externally constructed gold standard collected independently of the system itself. With two completed sessions logged at the time of writing, this project reports RQ1 as follows: the measurement mechanism exists, is real, and is live in production, but the sample size available at the time of writing is too small to report a word error rate or character error rate figure against the pre-registered threshold with any confidence that the figure would be stable if even a handful more sessions were added. RQ1 is therefore recorded as data collection pending, to be completed in a later iteration once a larger set of pharmacist-confirmed sessions exists, rather than as a pass or fail verdict against the 0.15 and 0.10 thresholds set in Chapter 3.

4.5 RQ2 — Recommendation Quality

The evaluation harness built for RQ2 computes precision at three and recall at three by ranking the pharmacist-judged gold-standard candidates against each other and checking where the valid ones land in that ranking, rather than by taking the system's own live, independently generated candidate list and checking it against the gold standard. Because pharmacist-valid candidates are placed ahead of invalid ones by construction before the ranking even begins, and precision and recall at a fixed cut-off are measured only by which candidates are in the top few regardless of their internal order, re-ordering the valid candidates among themselves by a secondary structural-similarity signal cannot change the resulting precision or recall figure at all. Put plainly, the two conditions the harness was built to compare, with and without the chemical-similarity signal included, are mathematically guaranteed to produce identical precision and recall numbers, so presenting them as a comparison would not be reporting a finding; it would be reporting an artefact of how the metric was constructed.

What the harness does measure genuinely, and what does differ between the two conditions, is the mean structural-similarity coverage among the valid candidates, a measure of how much chemical substructure the recommended alternatives actually share with the prescribed medicine, independent of whether that coverage changes their rank. As with RQ1, however, this figure depends on a pharmacist-judged gold standard of relevant and irrelevant candidates per case, and at the time of writing no such gold-standard judgements had been recorded against the one confirmed evaluation case in the live system. RQ2 is therefore also recorded as data collection pending: the measurement mechanism for structural-similarity coverage is built and understood, the precision-and-recall comparison between conditions as currently constructed would not produce a meaningful finding regardless of sample size, and a future iteration of this work should report structural-similarity coverage instead, once gold-standard judgements have been collected by a pharmacist reviewer, rather than the precision-and-recall comparison originally planned.

4.6 RQ3 — Evidence Grounding

RQ3 compares retrieval strategies, keyword-based retrieval against the FDA label corpus actually used in the live recommendation path, and a separate, embedding-based retrieval mechanism built but held behind a disabled-by-default configuration flag, and measures citation coverage, an unsupported-claim rate, and a semantic-similarity score between a generated explanation and the retrieved source text it was built from. At the time of writing, the semantic-embedding retrieval condition requires a dependency and corpus not enabled in the evaluation environment used for this report, and no case in the live system had been run through either retrieval condition as part of a recorded evaluation run. RQ3 is accordingly also recorded as data collection pending.

It is worth stating a methodological point here regardless of sample size, since it will matter however much data is eventually collected: the semantic-similarity metric this project's own evaluation design specifies carries a known circularity risk, described in Section 3.6.1, whenever a generated explanation is built directly from the same passages the metric then compares it against. Any future figure reported against the 0.80 semantic-similarity threshold should be read alongside this caveat and not presented as a clean, independent measurement unless the explanation text is generated independently of the exact passages used as the comparison reference.

4.7 RQ4 — Explainability and Trust

RQ4 requires structured feedback from a panel of licensed pharmacists, collected under the ethical safeguards set out in Section 3.7, and institutional research ethics approval is a precondition for that data collection to begin at all. Neither the ethics approval process nor panel recruitment had been completed at the time this report was written. Consistent with this project's own integrity rule, stated repeatedly through this chapter because it is the single most important methodological commitment the project makes, RQ4 is recorded here as not conducted rather than estimated, assumed, or illustrated with a small informal sample collected outside the approved process. This is a genuine, acknowledged gap in the work, discussed further as a limitation in Chapter 5, and not a result dressed up to look like one.

What can be reported for RQ4 is that the apparatus the question depends on is built and operational, which is a meaningful partial result in a Design Science Research framing even though it is not an answer to the question itself. The three explanation conditions described in Section 3.5.2 are implemented as a working counterbalanced assignment mechanism, so that a participant can be allocated to the score-only, score-plus-attribution, or score-plus-attribution-plus-provenance condition in a controlled order rather than an arbitrary one. The response-handling path strips free-text and identifying fields before storage, implementing the pseudonymisation safeguard promised in Section 3.7 rather than merely asserting it. The aggregation logic computes means, standard deviations, and per-construct breakdowns by condition, and reports an explicit insufficient-sample status rather than a misleading zero when no responses exist, which is the same availability-status discipline applied across the other three research questions. In other words, the gap in RQ4 is entirely a gap in data collection and institutional approval, not in the instrument or the software: were ethics approval granted and five pharmacists recruited tomorrow, the measurement itself could proceed without further development work. That distinction matters for the recommendations in Chapter 5, because it determines whether the remaining work is a research-governance task or an engineering one.

4.8 Discussion

Taken together, the four research questions describe a project whose build phase substantially succeeded and whose evaluation phase had not yet reached the scale the original study design called for by the time this report was due. That gap is worth discussing directly.

The worked example in Section 4.3 is useful here precisely because it shows what a full RQ2-style evaluation would need to be run at scale to answer the question the research design actually poses. A single query returning a sensible, well-evidenced top candidate is reassuring but is not the same claim as "the system ranks relevant alternatives ahead of irrelevant ones across a representative range of prescriptions," which is what RQ2 asks. The difference between these two claims is exactly the difference between a demonstration and an evaluation, and it is worth being explicit that this project, as submitted, has firm evidence for the former and not yet for the latter. The same point applies to the catalogue-scale figures in Section 4.2: a catalogue of over 900,000 product records is a real and substantial piece of engineering work, but its scale says nothing on its own about whether the recommendations drawn from it are clinically sound in aggregate, which only the pending RQ2 evaluation, once a gold standard exists, could actually establish.

It is also worth returning to the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 and asking, how well the system as built actually honours it. Section 2.4's discussion of therapeutic equivalence argued that regulatory determination should be the primary substitution signal and chemical similarity only a subordinate one; the worked example shows this holding exactly as intended on the different-ingredient path, where the Orange Book annotation for naproxen was attached as supporting evidence and played no part in ranking naproxen above the other four candidates. Section 2.5's discussion of interpretable-by-design models over post-hoc explanation is borne out directly by the feature-attribution output in Section 4.3, which states the exact arithmetic behind the score rather than an approximation of it, exactly the property Rudin (2019) argues high-stakes systems should have, and a sharper property than the local, approximate attributions Ribeiro et al. (2016) and Lundberg and Lee (2017) describe for genuinely opaque models. Section 2.7's argument that a human checkpoint should sit before consequential processing, not after it, is reflected in the verification gate confirmed in Section 4.2 to be enforced at the server rather than only in the interface. Where the system falls short of its own stated design, chiefly the chemical-similarity bonus discussed in Section 4.9 below, this report says so in the same register it uses to describe where the design succeeded, rather than quietly downplaying it.

Three distinct causes sit behind it, and they are not all the same kind of problem. The RQ1 and RQ2 gaps are, in part, measurement-design problems rather than purely data-volume problems: even with a full twenty-five to thirty confirmed cases in hand, the RQ1 harness as currently built would still only produce simulated multi-engine numbers rather than real ones, and the RQ2 harness as currently built would still produce identical precision and recall across its two comparison conditions by construction. Collecting more data would not, on its own, fix either of these; the harnesses themselves would need further work, redirecting RQ1 toward the real single-engine production signal already described in Section 4.3, and redirecting RQ2's condition comparison toward the structural-similarity coverage metric that genuinely does differ. The RQ3 gap is closer to a pure configuration and data-collection problem: the mechanism exists and the circularity caveat is already understood, and what remains is enabling the semantic retrieval path and running cases through it. The RQ4 gap is an ethics and recruitment lead-time problem, not a code or measurement problem at all; the survey instrument, the consent materials, and the condition-assignment logic are all built and working, and what remains is the institutional approval process and the recruitment of five licensed pharmacists, neither of which a software project can shorten by writing more code.

This discussion matters for how the rest of this report should be read. Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 describe a system whose design draws carefully on the literature reviewed there, and the system that was actually built does, by direct inspection of its code, implement that design faithfully in almost every respect examined for this report. What this chapter adds is the honest further step: a design faithfully implemented is not the same thing as a design empirically validated, and this project, as submitted, has substantially completed the former without yet completing the latter. Chapter 5 returns to this distinction when discussing recommendations for the work that remains.

4.9 Known Limitations of the Current Implementation

Beyond the data-collection gaps discussed above, nine specific limitations in the current implementation were identified during this project and are recorded here plainly. Seven were identified during development and review of the implementation against the design principles in Chapter 2; the final two were discovered in the course of running the worked example reported in Section 4.3, which is itself an argument for exercising a system against real queries rather than reasoning only about its source code.

First, the chemical-similarity bonus described in Section 3.3.4 does influence candidate ranking on the same-active-moiety path, even though Section 2.4's review of the therapeutic-equivalence literature argues that regulatory determination should be the primary signal and structural similarity only a subordinate one. The bonus is correctly suppressed on the different-active-ingredient path, where applying it would risk implying an equivalence no regulator has determined, but its presence on the same-moiety path is a real departure from the stated design principle rather than a fully resolved implementation of it. This is recorded here rather than left unmentioned, because the distinction between evidence that informs a pharmacist's own judgement and evidence that silently shapes what the pharmacist sees first is close to the project's central safety claim, and the current implementation does not yet hold that line as cleanly on every path as the design intends.

Second, the evidence-retrieval layer is not entirely passive in the way the regulatory equivalence layer is. When no supporting label passage can be found for a candidate, the system withholds the narrative summary rather than generating one without support, which is a deliberate and defensible choice given the unsupported-claim risk discussed in Section 2.6, but it does mean the layer affects what a pharmacist sees rather than purely annotating a result that was already going to be shown regardless.

Third, the human-in-the-loop audit trail described in Section 3.3.2 is append-only by application convention, meaning no code path in the system currently issues an update or delete against it, rather than by a database-enforced constraint such as a trigger or a restricted permission grant that would make such a modification impossible even for a future change to the code. Adding that database-level enforcement is a well-understood, comparatively small piece of remaining work, and its absence does not indicate any known tampering; it indicates that the current guarantee rests on the discipline of the code as written rather than on the database itself.

Fourth, the nine-component scoring model implemented in the live system differs in structure from an earlier three-component formula described in an internal design specification document produced before this report. The divergence is structural, not merely a matter of adjusted weights; strength now operates as part of the mandatory eligibility gate described in Section 3.3.4 rather than as one of the nine graded, weighted components. This is a deviation from an internal specification document, not from the formally approved PGD project proposal submitted for this project, which specified only that the system compute a transparent, weighted relevance score and did not fix any particular number of components. The implemented nine-component model is, if anything, more granular and more individually interpretable than the originally sketched three-component version, but the divergence from the internal specification is noted here as something that should be reconciled with the project supervisor as an approved amendment, rather than left as an undocumented drift from an earlier internal plan.

Fifth, the semantic-similarity measure specified for RQ3 carries the circularity risk already discussed in Section 3.6.1 and again in Section 4.6: any future figure reported against it should be read alongside that caveat rather than as a clean, independent measurement, unless the generated explanation is produced independently of the exact passages the metric compares it against.

Sixth, the equivalence determinations the system draws on are specific to one regulatory jurisdiction, as discussed in Section 3.4.1. The system's equivalence evidence is correct for that jurisdiction and should not be assumed to transfer unmodified to a health system operating under a different regulator's determinations, Pakistan's own regulatory authority among them.

Seventh, one of the OCR engines available to the system is platform-sensitive in practice and does not run identically across every environment it was tested in during development; this constrains where a future multi-engine RQ1 comparison can actually be executed, and any such comparison should record which environment each engine's figures were measured in rather than presenting them as environment-independent.

Eighth, the mandatory eligibility filters do not gate as strictly as Section 3.3.4 describes, at least on the different-active-ingredient path. As Section 4.3 records, the route check accepted a candidate whose route string contained the prescribed route as one element among several, admitting an injectable product on the strength of an oral alternative presentation, and accepted a buccal route as matching an oral one. The dosage-form check, meanwhile, compared null values on both sides and so passed every candidate without discriminating. Both are correctable: the route comparison needs to require that the prescribed route be available as a usable administration route for the candidate rather than merely present in its route string, and the dosage-form comparison needs to treat a missing value on either side as a failure to establish compatibility rather than as a match. Until they are corrected, the description of these filters as hard gates overstates what they currently enforce, and this report's Chapter 3 description should be read with that qualification.

Ninth, the nine-component score discriminates between candidates more coarsely than its component count suggests. Because each component awards either its full weight or nothing, candidates failing the same subset of components receive identical totals, as all four lower-ranked candidates in Section 4.3 did. A genuine therapeutic alternative and an implausible one can therefore be presented to the pharmacist with the same score, which weakens the ranking's usefulness even though the score itself remains exactly explainable. Introducing graded awards for the components that admit degrees of match, rather than a binary award for all nine, would be the natural remedy and is recommended as future work in Chapter 5.

None of these nine limitations is expected to prevent the project from being completed to a satisfactory standard. They fall into three groups. Two of them, the fifth and the seventh, concern how a future result should be read or where it can be measured, rather than obstacles to measuring it at all. Four, the third, sixth, eighth and ninth, are well-understood pieces of remaining engineering work with known remedies. The remaining three, the first, second and fourth, are design questions about where the boundary between evidence and instruction should sit, and these are the ones that warrant discussion with the supervisor rather than simply being coded away. They are recorded here in full because a report that presented only the parts of the system that worked as intended, and omitted the parts that did not, would give an inaccurate account of where the project actually stands.
