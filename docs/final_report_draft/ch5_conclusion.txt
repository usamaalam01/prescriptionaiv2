CHAPTER 5: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Conclusions

This project set out to build and evaluate MediScan, a human-in-the-loop, explainable decision-support prototype intended to help a pharmacist read a prescription correctly and identify a therapeutically relevant alternative when the prescribed medicine cannot be supplied. The build phase of that aim was substantially achieved. A working application exists, is deployed publicly, and runs the full pharmacist workflow, upload, multi-engine OCR extraction, mandatory field-by-field verification, recommendation, regulatory-equivalence annotation, evidence retrieval, and explanation, end to end against a real drug-knowledge catalogue of over nine hundred thousand product records assembled from FDA and DrugBank sources. The verification gate at the centre of the system's safety model was confirmed, by direct inspection of the code that enforces it, to be a genuine server-side constraint rather than a cosmetic one, and the recommendation engine's scoring model was confirmed to be exactly what Chapter 3 describes: nine independently weighted components summing to one hundred points, with an explanation layer that states the arithmetic behind each score exactly, without approximation.

The evaluation phase of the project's aim was not completed to the scale the original study design called for. Of the four research questions set out in Chapter 3, none could be answered with a pass or fail verdict against its pre-registered threshold by the time this report was written, because the evaluation dataset behind them, a planned twenty-five to thirty pharmacist-confirmed synthetic prescription cases, stood at one case at the time of writing, and the institutional ethics approval and panel recruitment RQ4 depends on had not been completed. Two of the four research questions, moreover, carry a measurement-design problem that additional data collection on its own would not resolve: the RQ1 evaluation harness currently produces simulated rather than real multi-engine OCR comparisons, and the RQ2 harness's two comparison conditions are, by the mathematics of how the comparison is built, guaranteed to produce identical precision and recall figures regardless of how much data is collected.

This project's single clearest finding, read across the evidence actually in hand, is a disciplined demonstration that the design described in Chapter 3 can be built and does hold together end to end, rather than a validated claim that it performs well against real-world prescriptions at the scale a deployed clinical tool would need. That is a narrower conclusion than the project originally set out to reach, and this report states it as such rather than dressing a partial result up as a complete one. Whether that narrower conclusion still represents a worthwhile contribution is, in the end, for the reader and the examining committee to judge; this report's responsibility was to state plainly what was found and what was not, which it has attempted to do throughout.

5.2 Impacts

Within the scope actually achieved, the project demonstrates that the five technical strands reviewed in Chapter 2, prescription OCR, regulatory therapeutic equivalence, retrieval-grounded evidence, explainable scoring, and a structurally enforced human-in-the-loop gate, can be integrated into a single working system rather than remaining five separate research threads that happen to appear in adjacent papers. The worked example in Section 4.3 shows this integration operating end to end on a real query against a real catalogue: an eligibility filter, a nine-component transparent score, a passive regulatory annotation, and a retrieved evidence passage all contributed to one traceable, explainable result, subject to the candidate-plausibility and filter weaknesses recorded as the eighth and ninth limitations in Section 4.9. Demonstrating that this combination is buildable at all, and documenting where parts of it still fall short of the design's own stated principles, is itself a genuine, if modest, contribution to the research gap identified in Section 2.8. It is worth adding that those shortfalls were found by inspecting the system's own component-by-component output, which is an argument in favour of the interpretable design the project adopted: an opaque ranker returning the same five candidates would have offered no comparable way to discover why.

5.3 Future Outlook

The most direct next step is completing the evaluation this report could not: constructing the full twenty-five to thirty case synthetic dataset, obtaining pharmacist-confirmed ground truth across it, and running RQ1 through RQ3 to a real pass or fail verdict against the thresholds set in Chapter 3. Doing so credibly requires two pieces of further engineering work alongside the data collection itself: redirecting RQ1 toward the real, single-engine production accuracy signal already confirmed working in Section 4.2, rather than the currently simulated multi-engine harness, and redirecting RQ2 toward the structural-similarity coverage metric that genuinely differs between conditions, rather than the precision and recall comparison that cannot. RQ4 depends on a separate, non-technical next step, institutional ethics approval and the recruitment of five licensed pharmacists, and should be planned for accordingly, since neither can be shortened by further development work on the application itself.

Two corrections should be treated as more urgent than the rest, because they affect what a pharmacist is actually shown. The eligibility filters need to gate as Section 3.3.4 describes: the route comparison should require that the prescribed route be a usable administration route for the candidate rather than merely appear somewhere in its route string, and the dosage-form comparison should treat an absent value on either side as a failure to establish compatibility rather than as a match. Alongside that, the scoring model should award graded rather than binary credit for those components that admit degrees of match, so that a genuine therapeutic alternative is not presented with the same score as an implausible one. Together these two changes would have excluded or demoted the implausible candidates discussed in Section 4.3, and neither is a large piece of work.

Beyond completing the evaluation as originally planned, two further directions follow naturally from the remaining limitations recorded in Section 4.9. Reconciling the chemical-similarity bonus's effect on same-moiety ranking with the principle that regulatory equivalence should be the primary signal, either by removing the bonus from the ranking calculation and presenting it purely as annotation, or by formally documenting and justifying its inclusion with the project supervisor's sign-off, would close the single limitation this report considers closest to the project's central safety claim. Enforcing the human-in-the-loop audit trail's append-only property at the database level, through a trigger or a restricted permission grant, rather than relying only on the discipline of the application code, would convert a currently procedural guarantee into a structurally enforced one.

5.4 Lessons Learned

Several lessons from this project are worth recording for their own sake, independent of the specific technical findings above. Pre-registering research questions, metrics, and acceptance thresholds before any evaluation was run, as Chapter 3 describes, surfaced two genuine design problems in the RQ1 and RQ2 harnesses before a single real result had been produced, which is a considerably better outcome than discovering those same problems after reporting numbers that later had to be retracted or re-explained. Separating the build phase from the evaluation phase as explicitly as the Design Science Research paradigm encourages (Peffers et al., 2007) made it possible to state clearly, in this report, exactly which phase the project had and had not completed, rather than blurring the two together into a single ambiguous account of progress. Building the evaluation infrastructure, the RQ1 through RQ4 harnesses, the availability-status model that distinguishes a genuinely unmeasured metric from a measured zero, and the audit trail that supplies ground truth, as early and as seriously as the pharmacist-facing application itself turned out to matter more than expected; it is the reason this report can state precisely why certain results are unavailable rather than only that they are.

5.5 Limitations of the Study

The limitations specific to the current software implementation are recorded in full in Section 4.9 and are not repeated here. Beyond those, three limitations attach to the study as a whole, as distinct from the artefact it produced. First, no quantitative result in this report is based on a dataset larger than a single confirmed case; every number this report could have reported against a pre-registered threshold is instead recorded as pending, and a future iteration completing that data collection may find results that differ materially, in either direction, from what a reader might reasonably guess from the system's design alone. Second, the qualitative RQ4 strand was not conducted at all, so this report contains no empirical evidence, of any kind, about how a working pharmacist actually experiences the system's explanations or whether those explanations genuinely affect trust in the way Tonekaboni et al. (2019) and the wider clinical explainability literature discussed in Section 2.5 suggest they might. Third, every synthetic prescription and evaluation case referenced anywhere in this project is synthetic by design, and the system's behaviour on the far more varied handwriting, abbreviation conventions, and clinical phrasing found in genuine prescriptions from routine practice remains untested.

5.6 Recommendations

For any continuation of this project, the single highest-priority recommendation is to complete the evaluation dataset before attempting to draw any further conclusion about the system's real-world performance; a well-built application whose claims rest on one data point is not yet a validated one, however carefully the application itself was constructed. Closely behind that, redirecting the RQ1 and RQ2 measurement approach along the lines set out in Section 5.3, rather than continuing to run the currently simulated and self-referential harnesses at larger scale, would avoid investing further data-collection effort into a measurement that cannot answer the question it was designed to answer regardless of sample size. Obtaining ethics approval for the RQ4 pharmacist panel should begin as early as institutionally possible, given its lead time is independent of any other work on the project. Finally, for any reader or future developer extending this codebase, the project's own integrity rule, that a metric is reported only when real data backs it and is otherwise marked unavailable rather than estimated, is recommended as a standing practice worth preserving beyond this report, since it is the single methodological commitment that made this chapter possible to write honestly.
