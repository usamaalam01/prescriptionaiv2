CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 General

This chapter reviews the body of work this project builds on. The review is organised around the five technical strands the system combines: prescription reading, therapeutic equivalence, explainable AI, evidence grounding, and human-in-the-loop design. It closes by identifying the gap that motivates the project. The aim is not an exhaustive survey of each field but an account of what is settled, what remains contested, and where this project fits.

2.2 Literature Collection

Literature was collected from Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library. Search terms combined clinical and technical vocabulary: handwritten prescription recognition, medication error OCR, therapeutic equivalence, generic substitution, explainable AI in healthcare, clinical decision support and trust, retrieval-augmented generation in clinical settings, and human-in-the-loop machine learning.

Preference was given to peer-reviewed work from roughly the last fifteen years, with older sources retained where they remain foundational, such as early accounts of medication-error causation. Regulatory and standards documentation was treated as primary source material rather than secondary literature, since the equivalence determinations this project relies on are regulatory artefacts rather than research findings in the conventional sense. Sources were screened first by title and abstract, then by full text against relevance to one of the five strands below.

2.3 Prescription Reading and Optical Character Recognition

Optical character recognition on printed text is a mature technology; commercial systems routinely exceed 99% character accuracy on clean, typed documents. Handwriting is a substantially harder problem, and medical handwriting harder still: prescriptions are written quickly, often use abbreviations and Latin-derived notation, and are produced under real time pressure in a clinical setting rather than in the controlled conditions under which most handwriting datasets were collected.

Research in this area has moved through several distinct phases. Early approaches relied on hand-engineered feature extraction (stroke direction, loop counting, character segmentation heuristics) combined with classical statistical classifiers. Later work applied convolutional and recurrent neural architectures to the sequence-recognition problem, treating a line of handwriting as a sequence to be decoded rather than a set of isolated glyphs to be classified one at a time (Graves et al., 2009). More recent work uses transformer-based models pretrained on large corpora of handwritten text, which have improved recognition accuracy on general handwriting considerably; the TrOCR architecture, which pairs a vision transformer encoder with a text transformer decoder and trains the pair end to end on large synthetic and real handwriting corpora, is a representative example and is one of the engines this project's own OCR pipeline draws on (Li et al., 2023).

Within the specific domain of medical and pharmaceutical handwriting, the literature is thinner and the reported accuracy figures are correspondingly more cautious. Studies that have attempted automated reading of handwritten prescriptions and clinicians' cursive medical notes report substantially lower accuracy than general handwriting benchmarks would suggest, precisely because medicine names are a closed but very large vocabulary of often-unfamiliar words, many of which differ from one another by a single syllable or letter (Jain et al., 2021; Tabassum et al., 2021). This is the look-alike, sound-alike problem named in Chapter 1, and it interacts badly with ordinary OCR error patterns: an engine that is otherwise highly accurate on English prose can still select the wrong member of a look-alike pair, because nothing in a general-purpose language model penalises that specific kind of mistake.

Two observations from this literature shape the present project. The first is that no single engine performs uniformly well across all handwriting; reported accuracy varies substantially with writing quality, image capture conditions, and domain, which is the practical justification for building a system around more than one OCR engine rather than committing to whichever engine tests best on a single benchmark. The second is subtler. The standard metrics used to report OCR performance, word error rate and character error rate, measure how faithfully characters were transcribed; a transcription can score well on both metrics and still name the wrong medicine, because a one-character substitution between two look-alike drug names costs almost nothing on a character-level metric while being clinically catastrophic. Metrics built for general text recognition do not, on their own, capture the thing that actually matters in this domain.

Both observations point toward the same design conclusion. A National Library of Medicine workshop on the state of clinical natural-language processing catalogued accuracy limitations of exactly this kind as a persistent, unresolved barrier to deploying automated text extraction directly in clinical workflows without a human checkpoint (Friedman et al., 2013). Read alongside the human-in-the-loop literature discussed in Section 2.7, the practical conclusion drawn for this project is that OCR output in a clinical setting is best treated as a proposal a human confirms before anything is done with it, not as a finished transcription acted on directly. This motivates both the multi-engine approach taken in this project and, more importantly, the mandatory verification step described in Chapter 3.

2.4 Therapeutic Equivalence and Medicine Substitution

Therapeutic equivalence has a specific regulatory meaning that is easily confused with looser, more colloquial notions of similarity. In the United States regulatory framework, products are considered therapeutically equivalent when they are pharmaceutically equivalent, meaning the same active ingredient, dosage form, route, and strength, and can be expected to produce the same clinical effect and safety profile when administered to patients under the conditions specified in the labelling. The determination is published by the regulator in a reference commonly known as the Orange Book, formally the Approved Drug Products with Therapeutic Equivalence Evaluations, which assigns a two-character equivalence code to each marketed product (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2024).

This matters for system design in a way that is easy to get wrong if the distinction is not taken seriously. Equivalence is determined by a regulator, as the outcome of an administrative and sometimes experimental process (such as bioequivalence testing); it cannot be read off a molecule's structure, however similar two compounds appear on paper. Two molecules may be structurally very similar without being clinically interchangeable, and conversely, products may be rated equivalent by the regulator despite differing in excipients or manufacturing process, because those differences were judged not to affect clinical performance. A system that inferred equivalence purely from structural similarity, without reference to an actual regulatory determination, would be making a clinical claim it has no authority to make.

The equivalence coding scheme also carries internal distinctions that must be respected if it is to be used correctly. Codes beginning with "A" indicate the regulator considers the product therapeutically equivalent to a chosen reference product, but these A-rated codes are further subdivided (for instance AB1, AB2, AB3) where more than one reference standard exists for the same active ingredient and strength; products rated against different reference standards within that subdivision scheme are not automatically interchangeable with one another, only with their own reference. Treating an entire top-level code letter as uniformly interchangeable, ignoring the subletter structure, would overstate what the regulator has actually determined, and a system built on this data has to preserve that distinction rather than flatten it away for convenience.

The literature on generic substitution more broadly examines its economic benefits (lower cost to patients and health systems with no loss of clinical effect in the great majority of cases) and its clinical safety. A large systematic review of cardiovascular generic-brand equivalence studies found little evidence of brand superiority across most of the drug classes examined, including narrow-therapeutic-index agents such as warfarin, but the review's own framing singles out narrow-therapeutic-index medicines, where the margin between an effective dose and a harmful one is small, as the subgroup meriting the closest individual scrutiny even where the aggregate evidence is reassuring (Kesselheim et al., 2008). Pharmacist-level survey work on narrow-therapeutic-index substitution specifically confirms that this caution persists in practice: many pharmacists report applying additional checks before substituting these agents even where regulatory equivalence has been established (Sarpatwari et al., 2018). Related work on patient- and prescriber-level attitudes toward substituted products finds that distrust of generic substitution persists among both groups even where the clinical evidence does not support that distrust, which is itself a reason an automated recommendation needs to show its evidence rather than simply assert an outcome (Colgan et al., 2015). Taken together, this body of work supports the position that substitution is beneficial in aggregate but is not universally appropriate in every individual case, which places the final decision with the professional rather than with an automated rule, exactly as this project's own scope insists on in Chapter 1.

For this project the conclusion drawn from the literature is explicit: regulatory equivalence ratings are treated as the primary evidentiary signal, chemical structural similarity is treated as a subordinate, supporting one, and neither is presented to the pharmacist as an instruction to act. Section 4.3 records where the current implementation does not yet hold that line as cleanly as the design intends.

2.5 Explainable AI and Clinical Trust

The interpretability of machine learning models has become a substantial research field in its own right over the past decade, driven in part by the recognition that opaque models are difficult to deploy responsibly in regulated and safety-critical domains, medicine prominent among them.

Two model-agnostic techniques recur throughout this literature. LIME, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations, approximates a model's behaviour in the local neighbourhood of a specific prediction with a simple, interpretable surrogate model, typically a sparse linear one, and reports which input features drove that particular prediction (Ribeiro et al., 2016). SHAP, SHapley Additive exPlanations, draws on cooperative game theory, treating each input feature as a player contributing to a prediction, and computes an attribution with desirable consistency and local-accuracy properties that LIME's purely local approximation does not guarantee (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). Both techniques produce per-prediction feature attributions, a list of which inputs contributed how much to a particular output, rather than a single global description of the model.

The clinical literature on explainability is considerably more ambivalent than the purely technical literature that produced these tools. Several authors argue that explanation genuinely improves appropriate reliance on a system's output, helping practitioners recognise when to accept a recommendation and when to question it (Tonekaboni et al., 2019). Others caution that explanations can induce unwarranted confidence rather than well-calibrated trust: a plausible-looking explanation may persuade a clinician even when the underlying prediction is wrong, because the explanation addresses the feeling of having understood the reasoning rather than the correctness of the reasoning itself. There is also a recognised distinction, argued most forcefully by Rudin (2019), between models that are interpretable by construction, where the explanation is an exact account of how the output was computed, and complex models that receive post-hoc explanations, which are approximations of a process the explanation method did not itself perform. Rudin argues that high-stakes decisions should prefer the former wherever a model of adequate accuracy can be built that way, precisely because a post-hoc explanation can mislead about the real mechanism.

This project's scoring model is additive and transparent by construction: the final relevance score is a weighted sum of nine independently scored components, and the contribution of each component to the total is an exact arithmetic fact about how the score was computed, not an approximation of it. This places the design closer to Rudin's interpretable-by-design position than to the post-hoc-explanation position. Where SHAP is additionally applied over that additive model, as described in Chapter 3, its purpose is narrower than in the typical SHAP use case: because the underlying score is already linear and additive, the exact contribution of each feature is known analytically, and the library's output can be checked against that known-correct value as a verification step, rather than used as the only available account of an otherwise opaque calculation.

2.6 Evidence Grounding and Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Large language models generate fluent text but can state things that are not supported by any source, a failure mode usually called hallucination and one with obvious hazards in a medical context, where a fluent but unsupported claim about a drug's safety profile could cause real harm if acted on. Retrieval-augmented generation addresses this by retrieving relevant documents from a trusted corpus and conditioning any generated text on those retrieved passages, so that the output is anchored to material that can in principle be checked against its source (Lewis et al., 2020).

In clinical applications the retrieval corpus is typically authoritative reference material, approved product labelling being the obvious candidate, since it is both regulator-reviewed and the material a pharmacist would otherwise have to consult directly. Evaluation of such systems distinguishes retrieval quality (did the system find the right passages) from generation quality (did the resulting text accurately represent what those passages say), and an increasing share of the recent literature on retrieval-augmented systems considers specifically whether generated statements are actually supported by the retrieved passages, rather than merely reading fluently (Es et al., 2024).

It is worth noting that the retrieval half of this architecture does not have to be learned. The dominant approach in the recent literature is dense retrieval, in which both query and documents are embedded into a shared vector space and matched by nearest-neighbour search, and this is the mechanism the original retrieval-augmented generation work employs. Alongside it sits a long-established family of lexical retrieval methods, term-frequency weighting and its probabilistic refinements among them, which match a query to a document on shared vocabulary and weight terms by how discriminating they are across the corpus. Lexical methods are often treated as the baseline that dense retrieval improves upon, and on open-domain benchmarks they frequently are. Their advantages are nonetheless real in a setting such as this one: they require no embedding model, no index-building step and no accelerator at query time, they behave deterministically, and critically for a clinical tool, the reason a passage was retrieved can be stated to a pharmacist in terms of the words it shares with the query, which a dense retrieval score cannot straightforwardly offer. Where the retrieval corpus is a controlled technical vocabulary, as approved drug labelling is, with medicine names and clinical terms appearing in consistent forms, the vocabulary mismatch problem that motivates dense retrieval is also less severe than it is in open-domain question answering. The production retrieval path in this project uses a lexical approach for these reasons, with a dense retriever implemented alongside it and evaluated separately under RQ3, and Chapter 3 describes both.

Semantic similarity metrics based on contextual embeddings, BERTScore among them, are commonly used to compare a piece of generated text against a reference text by comparing their embeddings rather than their surface word overlap (Zhang et al., 2020). Semantic similarity is not the same thing as factual correctness, however, and the literature is clear on this point: a generated sentence can sit close to a reference sentence in embedding space and still assert something false, if the two sentences share enough vocabulary and topic without agreeing on the actual claim. This distinction matters directly for the present project's own evaluation design, since a semantic-similarity score computed against the same text a system's own explanation was built from risks measuring overlap rather than correctness, a circularity problem that Chapter 3 names explicitly as a constraint on how one of this project's own metrics should be read.

2.7 Human-in-the-Loop Clinical Decision Support

Clinical decision support systems have a long history in health informatics, and their evaluation literature contains a consistent, recurring lesson: technical accuracy does not by itself guarantee adoption or clinical benefit. Systems fail in practice when they interrupt an already busy workflow, when they generate an excessive number of low-value alerts, or when practitioners cannot see why a particular recommendation was made and therefore cannot judge whether to trust it (Bates et al., 2003).

Automation bias, the tendency of a human operator to over-rely on an automated system's output and under-apply their own independent judgement, is a well-documented risk in safety-critical automated decision support generally, not only in healthcare (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Alert fatigue, where a high frequency of low-value notifications leads a practitioner to dismiss important ones along with the unimportant majority, is a related and separately documented risk specific to clinical alerting systems (Ancker et al., 2017). Both findings argue for systems designed to present evidence for a professional's own consideration rather than to issue directives the professional is expected to simply follow.

Human-in-the-loop design responds to these findings by placing a mandatory human decision point somewhere in the processing chain, but the literature is clear that where that point is placed matters as much as whether it exists at all. A confirmation step placed after the system has already produced and displayed its output invites what is sometimes called rubber-stamping: the human nominally reviews the output but has already been anchored by seeing it, and tends to confirm rather than independently re-derive the answer. A confirmation step placed before any consequential processing begins forces the human's own judgement to condition everything that follows, rather than merely to ratify a conclusion the system has already reached. The present project adopts the latter position: no recommendation is generated until the pharmacist has confirmed the extracted prescription fields, a design decision described in full in Section 3.3.2.

2.8 Research Gap

The five strands reviewed above are each reasonably well developed on their own, and pairwise combinations of them do appear in the literature, OCR combined with downstream NLP, or explainability combined with retrieval-augmented generation, for instance. What is not well represented is their integration into a single workflow with all of the properties this project targets together:

1. Prescription image to substitution recommendation as one pipeline. Work on prescription OCR generally stops at transcription. Work on medicine substitution generally assumes a structured, already-correct input. The join between the two, where a real system has to handle an OCR output that might itself be wrong, is thinly covered.

2. Regulatory equivalence as the primary substitution signal, rather than an afterthought. Systems that propose medicine alternatives in the wider literature frequently rely on similarity heuristics, often chemical or textual, as their only signal. Grounding recommendations in published regulatory determinations, with the subletter subdivision distinctions correctly respected, is markedly less common.

3. Explanation combined with evidence grounding, rather than either alone. Feature attribution explains how a model weighted its inputs; retrieved label text explains what an authoritative source actually says. These answer two different questions, and presenting both together, so a user can ask either "why did the model think this" or "what does the evidence actually say," is uncommon in the systems described in the literature.

4. Verification as a hard, structural gate rather than an optional overlay. Many systems in the clinical decision support literature offer human review as something the user can choose to engage with or skip. Enforcing confirmation as a precondition for any downstream processing, so that the system is structurally incapable of proceeding without it, is a stronger architectural commitment than most published systems make.

5. Integrity in how results are reported. Evaluation sections in applied AI work do not always distinguish clearly between a metric computed on real data and a figure produced by simulation for illustrative purposes. Treating that distinction as a first-class, enforced property of the system itself, rather than as a disclosure made only in a methods section, is unusual.

This project addresses that gap by building and evaluating a prototype that integrates all five of these properties, and, in keeping with the fifth point above, by reporting in Chapter 4 on where that integration succeeded within the time available and where it did not.
