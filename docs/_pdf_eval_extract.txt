A.  The Specification  
A1.  Research Context  
Medication errors are one of the persisting patient safety issues in healthcare throughout the world. In the 
United States alone, around 7,000 people die annually due to illegible handwritten prescriptions and/or 
drug-name confusion, with 11.4 percent of errors directly related to unreadable prescriptions (Chalasani 
et al., 2023).  
During my research, I observed that pharmacists often find themselves in situations where the prescribed 
drug is not available and have to replace it with a drug that is therapeutically equivalent, which is 
compounded by the fact that there more than 253,000 drug formulations in India and an estimated 1.5 
million globally (Chumble et al., 2025). This substitution is done without any formalised decision support 
system and can lead to dosage confusion and patient non-adherence. 
Existing digital healthcare platforms including Drugs.com, Medscape, and WebMD provide drug 
information but struggles to interpret handwritten prescription images or perform salt-aware generic 
substitution. Furthermore, the majority of AI systems that offer recommendations operate as black boxes, 
which is considered the biggest challenge to adoption by healthcare practitioners (Abo El-Enen et al., 
2025; Tun et al., 2025).   
This highlighted the need for an intelligent system that can read prescription images, retrieve reliable 
medicine information, and explain recommendations using modern AI techniques such as Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI). 
The research is undertaken in the remote desk-based research environment in Karachi, Pakistan, by 
using digital tools and structured interaction through online platforms.  
To ensure GDPR standards for ethical compliance and data privacy, research carried out using synthetic 
handwritten prescriptions are used to simulate realistic clinical environment, without processing any 
patient records or personally identifiable information (PII). 
A2.  Problem Statement 
Existing digital health care platforms do not have a comprehensible, integrated digital solution that can 
enable: (a) the extraction of drug names and dosage from handwritten prescription images, (b) the 
recommendation of therapeutically equivalent generic drugs based on chemically rigorous methods, (c) 
the grounding of all recommendations by AI algorithms in trustworthy regulatory sources, and (d) the 
presentation of the decision with explainable and sufficient explainability to support and build trust with 
pharmacists for Human-in-the-Loop clinical governance. 
A3. Research Aim 
This research aims to design, develop, and evaluate an IT artefact, AI-powered pharmaceutical assistant 
prototype that can extract drug name and dosage from handwritten prescriptions image, recommend 
therapeutically equivalent medicines, and generate evidence-based explanations to support pharmacist 
decision-making.

A6.  Ethical Considerations 
The University of Liverpool Online Research Ethics Committee has given full ethical approval for this 
research (Application 18274 signed 29 April 2026; Dr Nazmul Hussain). The ethical principles are based 
on the concepts of Fairness, Accountability, Transparency and Explainability (FATE). 
Key provisions are summarised below. 
Participants: Since the prototype is designed to support the clinical decision-making process of 
pharmacists, a sample of five licensed pharmacists were selected from the researcher's professional 
network in Pakistan. There are no vulnerable groups involved, no children and no real patient records are 
involved. The participants are adult pharmacists with full capacity to give informed consent. They play a 
valuable role by serving as a “human-in-the-loop” safeguard by reviewing AI recommendations and 
anonymously reporting back via digital surveys.

A7.  Anticipated Outcomes  
This research is anticipated to contribute a novel multi-modal framework to the field of pharmaceutical 
and healthcare informatics by integrating four technologies not previously combined within a single 
clinically oriented prototype: transformer-based handwritten OCR, graph-based chemical reasoning using 
RDKit Maximum Common Substructure (MCS), Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)-grounded 
language generation, and hybrid inline explainability through SHAP, LIME, and source attribution. The 
proposed framework aims to reduce AI hallucinations while addressing the interpretability gap that are 
highlighted in literature review (Sadeghi et al., 2024; Tun et al., 2025). 
The anticipated outcomes include the development of a functional MVP prototype deployed on Hugging 
Face Spaces. This projects aims to establish baseline data on OCR accuracy, recommendation quality, 
and RAG performance, while also gathering feedback from licensed pharmacists about the system.

Methodology LLM and RAG pipeline applied to unstructured regulatory documents (SmPC files);

Key Finding Retrieving chemically detailed pharmaceutical information, such as salt/base variants with 
correct dosage relationships is significantly more challenging than extracting surface-level 
product identifiers. The study also demonstrated that BERTScore is an effective semantic 
evaluation metric and that general-purpose embedding models can outperform domain-
specific alternatives in retrieval tasks. 
Gap Analysis The study focuses primarily on regulatory data standardisation and information extraction 
rather than real-time clinical decision support. It does not provide a mechanism for 
therapeutic drug substitution or chemically aware recommendation generation within 
pharmacy workflows. 
Project Contribution This review directly contextualises the design rationale behind adopting RDKit Maximum 
Common Substructure matching in this project, where distinguishing between chemically 
related salt and ester forms demands a level of molecular precision that keyword or rule-
based retrieval cannot achieve. Their findings also support two key design choices in this 
project. First, their use of BERTScore as a reliable semantic evaluation metric justifies its 
use for assessing the RAG explanation module. Second, their observation that general-
purpose embedding models outperform many domain-specific alternatives supports the 
use of all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with FAISS for retrieval. Unlike Kadi et al. (2025), this project 
extends retrieval capabilities into active clinical decision support by integrating RAG-
grounded explanations with a salt-aware recommendation engine for real-time therapeutic 
drug substitution.

Nagendran, M., Festor, P., Komorowski, M., Gordon, A.C. and Faisal, A.A. (2023) 'Quantifying the

Medicine, 6(1), p. 206. 
Dissertation Question DQ4 — XAI / Trust 
Methodology Nagendran et al. (2023) conducted an experimental study involving 86 intensive care 
physicians to evaluate how AI recommendations and explanation interfaces influence 
clinical decision-making. Participants were exposed to four conditions: no AI support, 
peer recommendations, AI recommendations alone, and AI recommendations 
accompanied by feature-importance explanations. 
Key Finding The study found that clinicians shifted their prescribing decisions toward AI 
recommendations regardless of whether explanations were provided. Simple feature-
importance explanations did not significantly improve trust or influence beyond the 
recommendation itself. Additionally, perceived usefulness of explanations showed little 
correlation with their actual influence on clinician decision-making. 
Gap Analysis The study highlights the limitations of simple explanation interfaces but does not 
introduce a multi-modal explainability framework combining SHAP, LIME, and source 
attribution for pharmaceutical substitution. 
Project Contribution Their findings influenced the project explainability design strategy. Instead of relying 
solely on feature-importance displays, the system integrates multi-modal explainability 
including SHAP visualisations, LIME perturbation analysis, and OpenFDA source 
attribution cards to assist pharmacists with meaningful reasoning and evidence.

B2.  Data Structures, Algorithms, and Pseudocode 
Knowledge Graph Schema 
The salt-aware knowledge graph is a NetworkX directed graph (DiGraph) with the following node and 
edge types: 
Element Type Description 
Ingredient Node Active pharmaceutical ingredient (API); maps to DrugBank ingredient list 
Salt Form Node Specific chemical salt or ester variant of an API 
NDC Product Node Individual commercial product identified by National Drug Code 
Strength Node Dosage strength (e.g., 500 mg) 
Route Node Administration route (e.g., oral, topical) 
Dosage Form Node Physical form (e.g., tablet, capsule, solution) 
HAS_SALT Edge Ingredient → Salt Form 
IS_ACTIVE_IN Edge Salt Form → NDC Product 
HAS_STRENGTH Edge NDC Product → Strength 
HAS_ROUTE Edge NDC Product → Route 
HAS_DOSAGE_FORM Edge NDC Product → Dosage Form

Similarity Scoring Algorithm 
For each candidate generic product, the similarity score is computed as: 
Score = (Strength_score × 0.4) + (Metadata_score × 0.4) + 
(FormRoute_score × 0.2) 
 
RAG Pipeline 
OpenFDA bulk label ZIP files are parsed and chunked at 512 tokens with 50-token overlap across seven 
FDA sections (indications, dosage, warnings, adverse reactions, drug interactions, contraindications, 
clinical pharmacology). Chunks are embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and stored in FAISS IndexFlatL2. 
At query time, the top-5 nearest chunks by L2 distance are retrieved, deduplicated by distance threshold, 
assembled into a structured prompt, and submitted to Groq (temperature 0.0) with the instruction to 
produce 2–3 summary sentences followed by 4–6 evidence bullets, citing only information present in the 
retrieved text. 
XAI Pseudocode 
SHAP_i = coef_i × (x_i − E[x_i]) 
where coef_i is from sklearn.LinearRegression fitted on 44-sample Cartesian 
background, 
x_i is the actual feature value, and E[x_i] is its expectation over the 
background.
